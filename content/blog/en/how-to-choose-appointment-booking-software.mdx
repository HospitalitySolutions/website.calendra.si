There is no shortage of appointment booking software, and at first glance the options look alike. They all promise a calendar, online booking and reminders. The differences that only surface after three months of use lie elsewhere: in how the system calculates availability, what happens to your invoices, and what you actually end up paying.

This guide is a list of criteria rather than a recommendation. Use it during a trial, not before one.

## 1. Does it calculate availability correctly

This is the one criterion that can rule out a product on its own. Ask yourself everything the system has to account for before it offers a customer a free slot:

- the working hours of each individual employee, not just the business,
- holidays, sick leave and one-off absences,
- service duration, which may differ per employee,
- preparation or cleaning time between appointments,
- rooms or equipment a service occupies,
- existing appointments, including ones you entered manually.

If any of these is missing, you will be resolving double bookings by phone. Test it by creating two services of different lengths, two employees and one shared room, then look at what it offers. How Calendra calculates availability is covered in more detail on the [appointment calendar](/en/appointment-calendar) page.

## 2. Does it support how you take payment

Service businesses differ more than you might expect. Some require a deposit, some are paid on site, others sell visit packages. Check whether the software supports:

- payment on site with no online payment at all,
- a partial deposit as a percentage or a fixed amount,
- full payment up front for group sessions,
- packages or memberships that count visits down,
- gift vouchers.

If it does not support your model, you will end up keeping two sets of records.

## 3. Can it issue the invoice you need

Booking and invoicing are one process in practice. If the software separates them, you will be retyping data. Ask whether a completed appointment becomes an invoice with the service, price and client details carried over, and whether it supports [fiscal verification of invoices](/en/blog/fiscal-verification-of-invoices-slovenia) if you take cash payments.

It is also worth reading how [booking and invoicing connect](/en/invoicing-and-payments) in a single workflow.

## 4. What it really costs

The published plan price is rarely the whole cost. Build a realistic monthly estimate:

| Line item | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Base plan | How many users are included |
| Additional users | Price per user per month |
| SMS messages | Price per message and included volume |
| Payments | The payment provider's transaction fee |
| Modules | What is an add-on rather than part of the plan |
| Setup | Whether data import is billed |

With three employees and 300 SMS a month, two products with the same headline price often differ by a factor of two. For the full breakdown, see [how much does booking software cost](/en/blog/how-much-does-booking-software-cost).

## 5. Can you take your data with you

Your clients, appointments and invoices are your asset. Before you commit, check that you can export the client list with contacts, the appointment history and issued invoices yourself, in an open format, without raising a support request. If there is no export, changing software later becomes very expensive.

## 6. Can the customer reschedule without calling

Software where cancelling requires a phone call manufactures no-shows. Check that the confirmation message contains a reschedule and cancel link that works without a login, and that you can set a deadline after which it is no longer allowed.

## 7. Is it in your team's language

The person using the software is the receptionist, not you. An English-only interface means slower work and more mistakes. Check the language of customer messages too, along with date and currency formatting.

## 8. How support responds

During the trial, send one specific question and measure how long it takes to get a useful answer. That is the best available indicator of what you will get when something actually goes wrong.

## 9. Where the data lives and who processes it

If you handle health data or other sensitive information, you need a data processing agreement, a list of subprocessors and a clearly stated hosting region. Calendra publishes all of these [openly](/en/legal), which is a good test to apply to any vendor: if you cannot find them without asking, that itself tells you something.

## 10. Will you still be using it in three months

The best software is the one your team actually opens. During the trial, let the people who will use it every day drive it, not only the owner. If they go back to the paper diary after two weeks, it is the wrong tool regardless of the feature list.

## How to run the test in one week

On day one, enter your real services, employees and working hours. On day two, have everyone book through the public link. On day three, create an invoice. On day four, test rescheduling and cancelling. On day five, try to export your data. Whatever does not work in those five steps will not start working later.
